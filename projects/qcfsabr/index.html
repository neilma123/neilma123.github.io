A quantitative research project by Neil Mascarenhas

I wanted to know whether the smile was tradable.

This is an old research idea I finally finished: calibrate a SABR volatility surface in C++, compare it with real SPXW quotes, and see what happens after bid/ask costs. The answer is less exciting than the model—and more useful.

The spread changes the story

At the midpoint, the experiment looks mildly promising. Once a trade pays the ask and exits at the bid, the edge disappears. That comparison is the point of the page.

How I ran it

I kept the pipeline intentionally plain: one daily chain, one calibration, one pair of legs, and a next-day exit.

1

Build the smile

Select liquid European SPXW calls with 21–60 days to expiry and infer the forward from same-strike call/put parity.

2

Calibrate SABR

Fit alpha, rho, and nu with beta fixed at one using a transformed Nelder–Mead optimizer.

3

Trade residuals

Buy the most underpriced implied-volatility point and short the most overpriced point in the same expiry.

4

Neutralize and exit

Vega-match the legs, delta-hedge at entry, and exit at the next end-of-day quote under both execution assumptions.

Implementation details

Numerics

Hagan's lognormal SABR approximation, Black-76 price/Delta/Vega, and bounded parameter transforms.

Data quality

127 verified trading days, 34-column chains, bid/ask quotes, volume, open interest, implied volatility, and Greeks.

Reproducibility

Build with PowerShell and rerun the full experiment with run_backtest.ps1. The trade log is committed as a compact audit artifact.

Takeaway: a good-looking model residual is not automatically a trading signal. In this sample, the spread consumed the apparent edge. I left that negative result visible because it is the most honest part of the experiment.
